## Review

**Passage mapping — read for structure, not memorization.** The first read
isn't about absorbing every detail; you can always look back. It's about
building a mental map: what does each paragraph *do* (make a claim, give
evidence, raise a counterpoint, qualify, conclude)? Track the direction
words as you go — *however, moreover, although, in contrast, therefore* —
they're the passage's own table of contents for its argument's shape.

**Question-type triage — different questions need different approaches:**

| Question type | Approach |
|:--|:--|
| Main idea / primary purpose | Answer from the *whole* passage's arc — reject anything too narrow (about one paragraph only) or too broad (beyond what the passage actually covers) |
| Detail / lookup | Go back to the exact line. Never answer from memory or general impression. |
| Inference | Must be strictly supportable by the text — not "reasonable in general," but actually implied by what's written. Real-world plausibility isn't textual support. |
| Function/purpose of a sentence | Ask what job that sentence does *for the argument* (introduce a limitation, provide evidence, pivot to a counterpoint) — not what it says in isolation |
| Vocabulary in context | Re-read the sentence with your answer substituted. GRE vocab-in-context often tests a word's secondary or less common meaning, not the first definition that comes to mind |

**Wrong-answer trap taxonomy** — most incorrect choices fall into one of
four patterns; naming the pattern makes them faster to spot:
- **Too extreme** — absolute language (*always, never, all, none, only*)
  where the passage is actually measured or qualified.
- **Half-right** — correct about part of the claim, wrong (or unsupported)
  about the rest. The most common trap on hard questions.
- **Out of scope** — true in the real world, or even true-sounding, but
  never actually stated or implied by *this* passage.
- **Opposite** — reverses the passage's actual claim or direction, often by
  missing a "however" or a "vice versa."

## Strategy

Read: [Magoosh — GRE Reading Comprehension: Tips and Strategies](https://magoosh.com/gre/gre-reading-comprehension/) (~8 min).

**Key takeaway:** efficiency on RC is speed *and* accuracy together, not a
tradeoff between them — a solid first-read map means most questions take
under a minute because you already know where in the passage to look,
rather than re-reading the whole thing per question.

## Practice — two harder passages (8 questions)

> **Passage 1.** Central banks that target a fixed rate of inflation, such
> as 2% annually, implicitly forgive past deviations: if inflation runs at
> 4% for a year, the bank's only commitment is to bring it back down to
> 2%, not to compensate for the excess accumulated during the high-
> inflation period. Proponents of an alternative approach, price-level
> targeting, argue this forgiveness is precisely the flaw: because
> inflation-targeting central banks let bygones be bygones, the public
> cannot form confident long-run expectations about the price level
> decades out, since a string of small overshoots, individually forgiven,
> compounds into large, unpredictable long-run drift. Price-level
> targeting instead commits a central bank to returning the price level
> itself to a pre-specified trend line, which mechanically requires a
> period of below-target inflation after any overshoot, and vice versa
> after an undershoot. Critics of price-level targeting counter that this
> very feature — deliberately engineering a recession-adjacent low-
> inflation period to correct a prior overshoot — could prove
> destabilizing in exactly the moments when an economy is least able to
> absorb it, such as immediately following a demand shock that already
> pushed inflation above target. Whether the long-run predictability
> price-level targeting offers is worth this short-run rigidity remains,
> per most central bankers who have studied the question, an open one.

> **Passage 2.** For much of the twentieth century, the mass extinction
> that ended the Cretaceous period, eliminating all non-avian dinosaurs
> along with roughly three-quarters of the planet's species, lacked any
> single, well-supported explanation. The 1980 discovery of a thin,
> globally distributed layer of iridium-enriched clay — iridium being rare
> in Earth's crust but common in asteroids — dating precisely to the
> extinction boundary, provided the first strong physical evidence for an
> extraterrestrial cause, later corroborated by the identification of a
> matching impact crater off Mexico's Yucatán coast. This impact
> hypothesis has since become the dominant explanation, but it has not
> fully displaced a rival account centered on the Deccan Traps, a vast
> series of volcanic eruptions in present-day India that were already
> underway before the extinction and continued for hundreds of thousands
> of years afterward, releasing enough sulfur and carbon dioxide to
> plausibly disrupt global climate on their own. Rather than treating the
> two hypotheses as strictly competing, a growing number of researchers
> now propose that the asteroid impact may have intensified the Deccan
> eruptions already in progress — through seismic effects that increased
> volcanic activity — such that the extinction resulted from the
> combined, and possibly mutually reinforcing, effects of both events
> rather than either alone.
