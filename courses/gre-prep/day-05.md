# Day 5: Sentence Equivalence Strategy

**Date:** August 8, 2026 · **Time budget:** ~2 hours

Sentence Equivalence is the other half of Short Verbal (your confirmed
weak spot, 50% baseline). It shares Text Completion's "predict, then match"
approach, but has one property TC doesn't: the two correct answers have to
be near-synonyms of each other. That structural fact is the single biggest
lever for handling words you don't fully know.

---

## Hour 1 — Sentence Equivalence

### Review

**Format:** one blank, six answer choices, select exactly **two**. Both
choices must (a) fit the blank logically and (b) produce sentences with
*equivalent* overall meaning. No partial credit — one right, one wrong
scores zero, same as two wrong.

**The core method:**
1. Read the sentence, predict a word for the blank — same as Text
   Completion.
2. **Scan the six choices for synonym pairs** *before* checking fit. Most
   SE questions contain two or three plausible pairs among the six words.
3. Check which pair actually matches your prediction/the sentence's logic.
   That's your answer.

**The rule that matters most for words you don't know:** the two correct
answers *must* be synonyms of each other. So **any word among the six with
no synonym partner at all cannot be correct — full stop, regardless of
whether you know what it means or whether it "sounds right" for the
blank.** Spotting an unpaired word and crossing it off doesn't require
knowing its definition, only recognizing it doesn't match anything else on
the list.

**The trap:** ETS regularly includes a **"dummy pair"** — two real synonyms
of each other that fit the *sentence structure* but not its *meaning* (often
the exact opposite direction of what's needed). Finding a pair is necessary
but not sufficient; you still have to check it against what the sentence
actually needs before locking it in.

### Strategy

Read: [PrepScholar — 5 Top Tips for GRE Sentence Equivalence Questions](https://www.prepscholar.com/gre/blog/gre-sentence-equivalence-questions/) (~6 min).

**Key takeaway:** eliminate unpaired words first — it's free progress that
costs nothing even on words you've never seen — then spend your real
thinking time checking the remaining pairs against the sentence's logic,
not just their existence as a pair.

### Reading Comp rep

Another original passage, ~200 words — this one on a research-methods topic
close to your own field.

> In the early 2010s, a series of high-profile failures to replicate
> landmark findings prompted a period of methodological self-scrutiny
> within experimental psychology now widely known as the replication
> crisis. Large-scale collaborative efforts, in which dozens of independent
> laboratories attempted to reproduce previously published results under
> closely matched conditions, found that a substantial proportion of
> effects — by some estimates, nearly half — failed to reach statistical
> significance the second time around. Proposed explanations for this
> pattern range from the relatively mundane, such as underpowered original
> studies that overestimated true effect sizes, to the more troubling,
> including selective reporting practices in which only statistically
> significant results were submitted for publication. The crisis has since
> prompted reforms that would have seemed unusual a decade earlier:
> pre-registration of hypotheses and analysis plans before data collection
> begins, mandatory disclosure of all measured variables regardless of
> outcome, and journals that commit to publishing a study's results before
> knowing what those results are. Critics of these reforms sometimes argue
> that rigid pre-registration can stifle the kind of exploratory analysis
> that generates genuinely novel hypotheses; proponents counter that
> exploratory findings remain valuable so long as they are clearly labeled
> as such, rather than presented as confirmatory tests they were never
> designed to be.
