> **Passage 1.** Astronomers cannot observe most exoplanets directly; the
> objects are simply too faint relative to the stars they orbit. Instead,
> the transit method infers a planet's presence indirectly, by measuring
> the tiny, periodic dip in a star's brightness that occurs each time an
> orbiting planet passes between the star and a telescope. The size of the
> dip reveals the planet's radius, while the interval between dips reveals
> its orbital period. This method has a notable limitation: it only detects
> planets whose orbits happen to be aligned edge-on relative to Earth, a
> geometric coincidence that excludes the majority of planetary systems in
> any given survey. Radial velocity, a complementary technique, instead
> measures the faint gravitational wobble a planet induces in its star's
> motion, and can detect systems the transit method would miss entirely,
> though it is comparatively less precise at determining a planet's exact
> size.

> **Passage 2.** Historians once assumed that double-entry bookkeeping, in
> which every transaction is recorded as both a debit in one account and a
> corresponding credit in another, was invented as a straightforward
> technical improvement over earlier single-entry methods, adopted purely
> because it reduced arithmetic errors. More recent scholarship complicates
> this account. Merchant records from fourteenth-century Italian trading
> houses show that double-entry systems were adopted unevenly and often
> gradually, with some firms using hybrid methods for decades after the
> technique was first documented, suggesting the switch was not simply a
> matter of recognizing an obvious efficiency gain. Some historians now
> argue that double-entry bookkeeping's real advantage lay less in error
> reduction than in its capacity to produce a coherent, auditable narrative
> of a firm's financial position at any single moment — a capability
> increasingly valuable as trading partnerships grew larger and investors
> more numerous, and as those investors demanded an accounting of a firm's
> health that did not require trusting the bookkeeper's memory or
> reputation alone. On this view, the technique spread fastest not among
> the merchants with the most transactions to track, but among those whose
> businesses depended most heavily on outside capital, since it was
> precisely those firms that needed to make their finances legible to
> parties who had not personally witnessed each transaction.

> **Passage 3.** When a group of painters later known as the Impressionists
> first exhibited together in 1874, critical response was largely hostile,
> and the exhibition's now-famous name originated as an insult: one
> reviewer, mocking Claude Monet's hazy harbor scene titled *Impression,
> Sunrise*, dismissed the entire group as mere "impressionists," painters
> who captured fleeting visual sensations rather than the carefully
> finished, precisely detailed compositions that academic juries of the era
> expected. The label stuck, and the painters eventually adopted it
> themselves, but the criticism it originated from reflected a genuine
> institutional bias: the government-sponsored Salon, which controlled
> access to prestigious public exhibition and had rejected many of these
> same painters' works in prior years, judged paintings primarily on
> technical finish and adherence to historical or mythological subject
> matter, criteria the Impressionists' quick, visible brushwork and
> everyday subjects — train stations, cafés, ordinary landscapes —
> deliberately abandoned. What later generations came to see as the
> movement's central innovation, an interest in capturing transient light
> and momentary perception, was, to its first critics, simply a failure to
> finish the job.
