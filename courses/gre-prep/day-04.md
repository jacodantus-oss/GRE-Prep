## Hour 1 — Text Completion

### Review

**Format:** 1-3 blanks, each with its own set of answer choices (3 choices
for a single blank, 3 choices per blank for multi-blank). No partial credit
on multi-blank questions — every blank has to be right.

**The core method, in order:**
1. **Read the whole sentence first, ignoring the answer choices entirely.**
2. **Predict your own word for the blank** — even a rough 1-3 word
   paraphrase — before looking at the options. This is the single biggest
   lever here: picking from choices without a prediction means the
   fanciest-sounding wrong answer gets an unfair vote.
3. **Match your prediction's *direction* to the choices**, not necessarily
   its exact word. You're looking for the closest fit, not a repeat.

**Structural signal words** — these tell you the blank's direction before
you've even fully parsed the meaning:

| Signals | Direction | Examples |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Contrast | Blank opposes the rest of the sentence | however, although, despite, yet, but, ironically |
| Continuation | Blank agrees/extends the rest of the sentence | moreover, likewise, similarly, and, furthermore |
| Cause-effect | Blank follows logically from a stated cause | because, since, therefore, thus, as a result |

**When you don't know the word:** don't guess blind.
- **Word parts first** — prefixes and roots carry real information. *re-*
  (back/again), *mal-/mis-* (bad), *bene-* (good), *-phile* (loving),
  *-phobe* (fearing) get you most of the way to a word's charge (positive/
  negative) even with zero prior exposure.
- **Eliminate by polarity, not by definition.** You often don't need to know
  exactly what a word means to know it's positive when the sentence needs
  negative, or vice versa. That alone kills 1-2 choices.
- **Formality is a tell.** GRE-correct answers skew formal/precise. A
  casual-sounding option in a formal academic sentence is usually a
  distractor.

### Strategy

Read: [Magoosh — Tackling Text Completion GRE Questions: Tips and Techniques](https://magoosh.com/gre/tackling-text-completion-gre-questions-tips-and-techniques/) (~7 min).

**Key takeaway:** predict before you peek, every time, no exceptions — it's
the difference between testing your reasoning against the sentence and
testing your reasoning against five persuasively-written wrong answers.

### Worked examples — double-blank (untimed, no quiz — read the reasoning)

**1.** *Although the critic's review seemed (i)\_\_\_\_\_\_ at first glance
— full of measured praise and gentle qualifications — a closer reading
revealed a (ii)\_\_\_\_\_\_ dismissal of the film's central premise.*
- (i): A) laudatory B) cursory C) ambivalent
- (ii): D) scathing E) muted F) quixotic
- **Answer: (i) laudatory, (ii) scathing.** "Full of measured praise"
  defines blank (i) directly. "A closer reading revealed" is the contrast
  signal — appearance vs. reality — so (ii) must be strongly negative.

**2.** *The negotiator's (i)\_\_\_\_\_\_ demeanor put both sides at ease,
even as she (ii)\_\_\_\_\_\_ pursued concessions that neither party had
been willing to offer minutes earlier.*
- (i): A) placid B) impetuous C) laconic
- (ii): D) relentlessly E) half-heartedly F) cursorily
- **Answer: (i) placid, (ii) relentlessly.** "Put both sides at ease" means
  calm — placid. "Even as" signals contrast: outwardly calm, but internally
  forceful in pursuing concessions — relentlessly.

Notice both examples solve independently, blank by blank — nail (i) using
its own local context before even reading what (ii)'s options are.

### Reading Comp reps

RC starts today, short and recurring rather than concentrated on one day.
Original passage, ~200 words, ~4 minutes to read plus 3 questions.

> Clinical researchers have long recognized that a patient's expectation of
> improvement can itself produce measurable physiological change, a
> phenomenon most commonly discussed as the placebo effect. What is less
> widely appreciated, however, is that the strength of this effect appears
> to vary systematically with the perceived invasiveness of the
> intervention: a saline injection reliably outperforms a sugar pill in
> trials measuring subjective pain relief, even though neither treatment
> has any pharmacological effect. Some researchers have proposed that this
> gradient reflects a kind of learned association, in which the ritual and
> apparent seriousness of a medical procedure calibrate a patient's
> expectations more powerfully than a passive pill can. Others contend that
> the explanation is less about ritual than about attention: more elaborate
> interventions simply command more of the clinician's — and by extension,
> the patient's — sustained focus. Whatever the underlying mechanism, the
> finding complicates the design of clinical trials, since a true placebo
> control must now be matched not only in appearance but in perceived
> invasiveness, lest the comparison arm itself introduce a confound the
> study was meant to eliminate.
