## Part 1 — Algebra repass (~20 min)

### Factoring quadratics, rebuilt from scratch

For x² + bx + c (leading coefficient 1): find two numbers that **multiply
to c and add to b**. Sign pattern: if c is positive, both numbers share the
sign of b; if c is negative, they have opposite signs (the larger-magnitude
one takes the sign of b).

x² + 5x + 6 → need product 6, sum 5 → 2 and 3 → **(x+2)(x+3)**
x² − 2x − 15 → need product −15, sum −2 → −5 and 3 → **(x−5)(x+3)**

**When the leading coefficient isn't 1** (e.g. 2x² + 7x + 3), use the "ac
method": multiply a×c, find two numbers that multiply to that product and
add to b, then split the middle term and factor by grouping.

2x² + 7x + 3 → a×c = 6, need product 6 sum 7 → 1 and 6 → split: 2x²+x+6x+3
= x(2x+1) + 3(2x+1) = **(2x+1)(x+3)**

**Recognize these two patterns instantly, no derivation needed:**
- Difference of squares: a² − b² = (a−b)(a+b)
- Perfect square trinomial: a² ± 2ab + b² = (a±b)²

### Inequality sign-flips, the flip-proof way

The rule (flip when multiplying/dividing by a negative) is easy to state and
easy to forget mid-problem. Here's a way to **never need it**: instead of
dividing by a negative coefficient, move the variable term to the other
side by addition first, so you only ever divide by a positive number.

Compare the two approaches on the same problem, 12 − 4x > 0:
- **Standard way:** −4x > −12 → divide by −4, flip → x < 3.
- **Flip-proof way:** add 4x to both sides first: 12 > 4x → divide by
  positive 4 → 3 > x, same answer, zero flips to remember.

Use whichever clicks, but if you keep second-guessing yourself on flips,
default to rearranging so you're always dividing by a positive number.

## Part 3 — AWA: Issue Essay

### Review

**Format:** 30 minutes. You're given a general claim (and often a
supporting "reason") and asked to state your position and defend it.
There's no "correct" side — the grader scores reasoning quality, not which
side you pick.

**What the baseline essay (3.5/6, 39th percentile) got dinged for, exactly:**
repetitive and somewhat circular reasoning, no concrete examples, uneven
organization with awkward transitions, and a few grammatical errors. Every
part of today's template exists to fix one of those specifically.

### The template

1. **Intro** — paraphrase the claim in your own words (don't just repeat
   it), then state a clear, *qualified* position: not "X is always true" or
   "X is always false," but something like "X holds in most cases, except
   when Y" — that qualification is what signals nuance to a grader, and it
   sets up your paragraph 3.
2. **Body 1** — your strongest reason, developed with **one specific,
   concrete example** — a real event, a named field/institution, or a
   detailed hypothetical with actual specifics (not "some people think...").
   Vague examples were exactly the baseline's problem.
3. **Body 2** — a second, *different* reason (not a rephrasing of body 1 —
   that's what "circular" means to a grader), with its own concrete example.
4. **Body 3 — the complexity paragraph.** Name the strongest counterargument
   to your position, take it seriously for a sentence or two, then explain
   why your position still holds on balance. This single paragraph does
   more for your score than almost anything else in the essay.
5. **Conclusion** — restate your position in new words (not copy-pasted
   from the intro), informed by the complexity you just addressed. 2-3
   sentences, no new content.

**Transitions, so paragraphs don't feel disconnected:** *However* /
*That said* / *Admittedly* (pivoting to a counterargument); *Furthermore* /
*Building on this* (adding a second reason without sounding repetitive);
*Consider the case of...* / *For instance* (launching into a concrete
example); *On balance* / *Ultimately* (concluding).

**Last 2 minutes: proofread.** The baseline essay had an actual typo
("Showinhg") that a 90-second read-through would have caught.

### Strategy

Read: [Magoosh — GRE Issue Essay: Strategies + 8 Real Student Essays with Scores](https://magoosh.com/gre/awa-issue-essay-strategies/) (~10 min, skim the scored examples).

**Key takeaway:** the gap between a 3.5 and a 4.5+ essay is almost never
grammar — it's concrete specificity and genuine engagement with complexity.
An essay that picks a side, gives one named example per reason, and
seriously grapples with a counterargument will consistently outscore a
grammatically cleaner essay that stays abstract and one-sided.

### Practice essay — timed, 30 minutes

> **Claim:** Public institutions — schools, libraries, and government
> agencies — should prioritize accessibility over efficiency, even when
> doing so increases costs and slows service delivery.
>
> Write a response in which you discuss the extent to which you agree or
> disagree with the claim. In developing and supporting your position,
> describe specific circumstances in which adopting the claim's position
> could be beneficial or harmful.

Set a real 30-minute timer before you start.
